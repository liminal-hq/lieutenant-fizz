# The Tauri app and the launcher

**Status: spike scaffolded.** `apps/player` exists as a spike: a menu, a capability probe and the Episode 1 build with a relative base, in a Tauri v2 shell with a tracked Android project and the app icons (see [TAURI_SPIKE.md](TAURI_SPIKE.md)); the launcher, shared options and everything else below are still planned. This document is the working plan for an Android and desktop app that plays every episode, and for the launcher that picks the episode. It follows the Claude Design export in `design/` (`Launcher.dc.html`, `Mobile Design.dc.html`, `App Icons.dc.html`) and is iterated in pull requests. `docs/STATUS.md` links here. Touch controls, the phone layout and haptics are in [MOBILE.md](MOBILE.md).

## Goals
- **One app for the whole series.** Episodes share the engine and are small (the art is generated in code, and the Episode 1 WASM is about 65 kB gzipped), so every episode is bundled in one install: one icon, one Settings screen, one place for saves.
- **A shared launcher that replaces each episode's title screen** when an episode is started from the app. Played from the website, an episode keeps its own title screen (and on a phone browser, the mobile title in MOBILE.md).
- **Reuse the Liminal HQ app pattern.** Cadence and Threshold are Tauri v2 Android apps with a React frontend, an `apps/<name>/src-tauri` layout, a tracked `gen/android` project and a side-by-side dev build. Fizz follows the same shape.

## Layout
```
apps/
└── player/                      the Tauri app
    ├── package.json  index.html  vite.config.ts
    ├── src/                     the React launcher
    │   ├── main.tsx
    │   ├── routes/              Carousel (episodes + App settings), Settings, Saves, About
    │   ├── components/          the game's menu language: scrim, stepped plate, soda cursor
    │   └── lib/                 episodes.ts, saves.ts, options.ts, launch.ts
    └── src-tauri/               Cargo.toml, build.rs, tauri.conf.json (+ tauri.conf.dev.json),
                                 capabilities/, icons/, src/, gen/android (tracked)
plugins/                         Tauri plugins authored in this repo (predictive-back, until it moves; see The Tauri shell)
assets/icon/                     icon sources (see Icon)
```
- The app depends on the engine and on the episode builds. Nothing in `crates/`, `packages/` or `episodes/` imports from `apps/`.
- `site/` stays static HTML: it must work without JavaScript, carry the social card and load fast. The launcher and the website share `site/episodes.json` and the Fizz fonts, not code.

## The launcher design
The launcher is a game-style screen, not a web page: the same menu language as the game (a soft scrim over art, a stepped plate that slowly cycles colour, a can of cream soda as the cursor), set in Fizz. Two directions were designed and **1a was chosen**: the launcher is the episode's title screen, so there is one layer and you go straight into play. The hub that hands off to each episode's own title menu (1b) was not chosen.

### The carousel
- **Episode stops.** A header reads `← Episode 1 · The Cocoa Caper →` with a pip per stop. Swipe the demo or tap the arrows to change stop, and the menu below follows. Each episode stop stands in for that episode's own title screen.
- **Menu rows:** Continue (with the source on the right, such as "Autosave" or "Slot 2"), New game, Load game, Options. An episode with no saves leaves out Continue.
- **Touch:** a tap moves the cursor to a row and chooses it, as on any Android list (the artboards show select-then-choose, which is replaced to match Android). **Keyboard and pad:** ↑↓ select, ←→ change episode or value, Enter plays, Esc goes back.
- **Behind the menu:** one tileable 640×144 demo strip per episode (sky, hills, crystals, platforms, pickups) with Ben running across it. It is pre-drawn sprite art, generated from the game's sprites (`design/melting/launcher-art.js` is the prototype of it), so **the launcher loads no WASM and no game code**.
- **Footer:** a hint bar in the same keycap style as the game's.

### The App settings stop
The last stop is App settings, with three rows:
- **Settings:** Sound, Display and Controls tabs, with a Reset button. The Sound tab carries Style (Classic or Enhanced, starting on Auto), Music and Effects, the same three settings as the game's Options > Sound screen (which also has a Sound lab switch; the app does not) (later also Night mode and Mono, 8c.5).
- **Saves:** export every episode's saves to one file and import them back. The file is `.fizzsave`, a versioned JSON bundle of each episode's autosave and slots, and is written through the Tauri dialog and fs plugins in the app.
- **About:** version, licences (the Fizz font is OFL-1.1), links. Tapping Version seven times unlocks the engine panel.

### Options model: global plus per-episode overrides
- Options are shared, and each row can be set for **All episodes** or for **Episode N**. A value that is overridden for one episode is marked `*`.
- Options opened from inside an episode get the per-row scope switch. Options at the launcher stop are global (there is no switch on the App settings stop).
- **Reset** needs a second tap within three seconds. On an episode it clears that episode's overrides, and in App settings it restores every default.
- **Storage (proposed):** one shared options key holds the global values, and each episode keeps a sparse overrides object. The current `lf-ep1-options-v1` is migrated into the shared key, with no overrides.
- **Touch settings** (size, opacity, hand, haptic strength and moved controls) belong to the device, not to an episode: they are in `lf-touch-v1`, reached on a touch device from Options > Touch controls (the Move controls row there is the drag editor) and, for the haptic strength, Options > Haptics, and fold into the shared key with the launcher work (the same migration as `lf-ep1-options-v1`). **Rumble** (the controller's strength, Off to Strong) and the Haptics lab switch are stored in `lf-ep1-options-v1` as `rumble` and `hapticsLab`, so they move with the options. The launcher's Settings > Controls tab shows the same rows.

### Desktop
The desktop window (1280×720) is the same carousel with a frameless title bar. Hovering a row selects it and a click chooses it, and Tab switches section. Desktop swaps the touch settings for its own:
- **Display** adds **Window** (windowed, fullscreen or borderless) through the Tauri window APIs, with F11 for fullscreen.
- Haptics becomes **Rumble**, driven by the same cue table through the controller (see [MOBILE.md](MOBILE.md#haptics)). On the web it is the Rumble row of Options > Haptics, shown once a pad that can rumble has been seen; the desktop app shows it from the start.
- **Controls** has keyboard layouts and shows the connected controller.
- App settings gets a **Quit** row.
- The fullscreen design (3840×2160) drops the title bar, so the launcher gets the whole 16:9 panel and the art scales up. It is shown at 4.8×, which is not a whole number: see "Pixel scale in the launcher" under Open questions.

#### Window, fullscreen and Quit
Built for the desktop app, except Quit to launcher, which Android has too; the web and iOS builds have none of it. The page finds out where it runs through `HostBackend` (`packages/engine/src/host.ts`): `createHostBackend()` reads `__TAURI_INTERNALS__` (or `isTauri`) the user agent and `navigator.maxTouchPoints` (an iPad in desktop content mode reports a Macintosh user agent with touch points, so it counts as `tauri-ios`), and gives `web`, `tauri-desktop`, `tauri-android` or `tauri-ios`. `quit()` is defined only for `tauri-desktop`, and `quitToLauncher()` for `tauri-desktop` and `tauri-android` (a web page has no launcher to go back to), and `main.ts` hands the backend to the game (`GameOptions.hostBackend`) the way it hands over storage.
- **Fullscreen** is the native window's. `nativeFullscreenBackend` (`packages/engine/src/native-fullscreen.ts`) implements `FullscreenBackend` over `getCurrentWindow().setFullscreen()` and `isFullscreen()`, follows the window's resize events for `onChange`, and sets `escapeCaptured` to true, so the Esc rules are the ones in `docs/MOBILE_PLAN.md` ("Built in the Esc and Pause keys in fullscreen"): the first Esc in a level pauses, Esc at the top of the pause menu or the title leaves fullscreen. The Tauri window API is imported lazily, so the web bundle is unchanged. The web Fullscreen button is hidden in the app (it is a browser-page control); `F` and `F11` toggle the window instead, and the hint bar and Controls table list `F`.
- **Options > Display > Fullscreen** appears in the desktop app with the same Auto, On and Off values, saved as `fullscreen`. It is read once, when the app starts: **On** starts the window fullscreen, **Off** never does, and **Auto** leaves the window as it opens (windowed), because a desktop has no "touch device" rule to follow. `?fullscreen` and `?fullscreen=off` still win over the saved choice. Changing the row does not move the window; it applies the next time the app starts. The row does not appear on the web, where it governs the browser's automatic request on a tap, or on Android, which is always fullscreen natively.
- **Quit game** is the last row of the title menu and the pause menu, shown only when `host.quit` exists, so never on the web, Android or iOS (a page cannot close its tab; Android leaves a game with Back and Home; at the bare title the system takes Back and backgrounds the app). It leaves fullscreen first and writes the store file out before closing; if the write fails (for example the disk is full) the game stays open with a "Couldn't save" message rather than losing the changes, and Quit game tries the write again. The title quits on one tap. The pause menu asks twice so a stray tap does not close a game with unsaved progress: the first tap arms the row, which then reads `Quit game  Tap again`, and the second within three seconds (the same `RESET_ARM_MS` as Reset on the Sound and Touch controls screens) quits; moving the selection, tapping another row, leaving the pause menu (Resume, a sub-screen, reopening it) or waiting disarms it. Esc and the Pause key keep their meaning and never quit.
- **Quit to launcher** is the row above Quit game (so the desktop menus end `Quit to launcher`, `Quit game`, and Android's ends with `Quit to launcher` alone), shown only when `host.quitToLauncher` exists, so never on the web or iOS. It follows Quit game's confirm: one tap on the title, two on the pause menu, where the first tap arms the row (`Quit to launcher  Tap again`) and the second within three seconds goes. Arming one of the two rows never arms the other, and the same things disarm it. On confirm the game writes the store file out; if the write fails (`flush()` resolves `false`, or rejects) it stays in the game with the same "Couldn't save" message Quit game shows, because leaving would drop what only the page's memory holds. Otherwise it disposes itself (the loop, input and audio stop) and navigates to the launcher with `location.assign`; the window stays as it is, fullscreen included, because the launcher lives in the same window. The destination is the one constant `LAUNCHER_URL` in `packages/engine/src/host.ts`, `../index.html`, relative to the episode page (`episode-1/index.html`), and it resolves to the same launcher on `tauri://localhost` and `http://tauri.localhost`. Today that is the spike menu `apps/player/index.html`; the launcher work changes the constant, nothing else. The embedded mode below ("Back to launcher" on `returnTo`) is the later, launcher-driven version of the same exit.
- **The quit command** is a small app-defined Rust command in `apps/player/src-tauri/src/lib.rs`, `quit`, that calls `app.exit(0)`, invoked as `invoke('quit')`. An app-defined command needs no capability entry, so it widens nothing; the process plugin would add a crate and an `allow-exit` permission any page script could use. The capability file adds only `core:window:allow-set-fullscreen` and `core:window:allow-is-fullscreen`.
- **Trying it without the app:** `?debug&host=fake-desktop` pretends to be the desktop app with a window that has no OS behind it, and counts Quit game in `window.__lfQuits` instead of closing the page and Quit to launcher in `window.__lfLauncherLeaves` instead of navigating; `?debug&host=fake-android` is the Android app, with Quit to launcher only. `e2e/desktop-host.spec.ts` and `e2e/touch-desktop-host.spec.ts` use it. The fake Android host also fakes the Back gesture (see Back peek). Not run in the real app: the window API, the `quit` command and the F11 key against a window manager have only been type-checked and built (`cargo check`), not exercised.

### Settings in scope
The designs show settings the game does not have today. In scope for the first app release:
- **Pixels: Sharp or Soft.** Sharp keeps whole pixels and shows a slightly taller view (more of the level) instead of bars. Soft ("Fill") stretches to the edges, a little soft. Touch defaults to Sharp and desktop to Soft; until the option is built, `?pixels=sharp|soft` sets it. A later display settings slice (6b in `docs/MOBILE_PLAN.md`) adds Auto, a render-scale step for slower GPUs and CPUs, and the choice on desktop.
- **Desktop window mode** and **controller rumble**, as above.
- **Scanlines** (a CRT scanline overlay) and **Screen shake** (on or off). Both are new presentation features, and Screen shake must stay out of the sim.

Not in scope for now: the **Language** row (the game has no localisation) and **V-sync** (a WebView cannot control it; a frame cap would be the nearest thing).

## Launching an episode
The launcher navigates the WebView to the episode's own page with a launch contract in the URL, for example `episode-1/index.html?launch=continue`. Each episode's `main.ts` reads it through a small `parseLaunch()` in `packages/engine`.
- **Values:** `continue` (the newest save, or the one the Continue row named), `new`, or `load` followed by a slot, matching the carousel's rows.
- **Embedded mode:** `Game.start` skips the title screen and goes straight to the requested start. "Quit to title" in the pause menu becomes "Back to launcher" and navigates to `returnTo`.
- **Normal mode:** with no launch parameters nothing changes, so the website's Play button still opens the episode's own title screen.
- Other ways to launch were considered and rejected for now: mounting the episode inside the launcher (needs a `mount`/`unmount` contract and careful WebGL and audio cleanup) and an iframe (focus, audio unlock, fullscreen and gamepad all get awkward).

## Shared storage
- **Where it lives:** in the app, every `lf-*` key (saves, options, touch settings) is kept in the `tauri-plugin-store` file `lf-data.json` in the app data directory, which the OS does not evict and which also suits desktop. On the web it stays in `localStorage`. The game talks to a storage adapter (`KeyValueStorage`, see ENGINE_SPEC section 4.4) and the entry point picks the backend before the game starts. The app starts with an empty store; nothing is copied over from an earlier `localStorage`.
- **Options:** see the options model above.
- **Saves** stay per episode (`lf-ep1-save-v1` and `lf-ep1-slot-1…4`). The launcher reads their metadata (location, lives, score, time played, date) as plain JSON, so Load game shows text rows such as "Slot 1 · Lives 4 · 8,150 pts · 0:41 played". The design has no thumbnails in the launcher, so no save-format change is needed for it.

## The Tauri shell
- The sim stays WASM inside the WebView. Calling a native sim over IPC every frame would cost more than it saves.
- **Plugins:** `predictive-back` (from Cadence and Threshold) for the Back gesture, and the haptics plugin (see [MOBILE.md](MOBILE.md#haptics)). The haptics plugin lives in `tauri-plugins-workspace` (`plugins/phone-haptics`) and is published as the `tauri-plugin-phone-haptics` crate (plugin id `phone-haptics`; version 0.1.0 in `apps/player/src-tauri/Cargo.toml`, resolved in `Cargo.lock`), so the lab and Fizz share one source. It was developed as `tauri-plugin-haptics`, so its commands are `plugin:phone-haptics|...` and the guest global is `window.__TAURI__.phoneHaptics`; the Rust type names and the Android package are unchanged. The game prefers it over `navigator.vibrate` inside the app once it reports a vibrator. A `plugins/` folder here is only for plugins authored in this repo.
- **Predictive back:** `plugins/predictive-back` is a copy of Cadence's `tauri-plugin-predictive-back`, authored here for now with its name and API unchanged, and it will move to the `tauri-plugins-workspace` repository once it settles. It is a path dependency of `apps/player/src-tauri` (excluded from the root Cargo workspace, like the app), registered in `lib.rs` on every platform (the desktop build is a no-op stub) and granted by `capabilities/predictive-back.json`. Its generated `permissions/` files come from its `build.rs`. The page side is `packages/engine/src/predictive-back.ts`, which loads the Tauri API lazily and exists only on the `tauri-android` host. On a completed gesture (`invoked`) it calls `Game.back()`, which does what Back means on the screen showing, as `backAction` says (the same rules the browser's Back button follows in fullscreen or installed mode, via `BackGuard`). `Game` tells the plugin `setCanGoBack(true)` whenever `backAction` has an answer for the current screen, and `false` on the bare title and while loading, so the system handles Back there and backgrounds the app. Quit to launcher also sends `setCanGoBack(false)`, and waits for it, before it leaves the page, because the native callback outlives the page's listener and the launcher has none. The `started`, `progress` and `cancelled` frames drive the **Back peek** (below). The Kotlin side now sends `swipeEdge` (`"left"` or `"right"`, from `BackEvent.swipeEdge`) with `started` and `progress`, as an optional field of the event: the Rust `PredictiveBackEvent` has an optional `swipe_edge`, the guest types an optional `swipeEdge`, and every existing consumer and the commands are unchanged. `cancelled` and `invoked` carry no edge. Not run on a device or emulator yet: the Kotlin unit test and the gesture itself need an Android build, which is done only in the container.
- **Back peek:** while the gesture is in progress on the pause menu or on any screen opened over the title or the pause menu (Controls, Options and Sound, Haptics, Display, Touch controls and Controller over it, Saves), the screen slides away and fades, revealing what Back goes to behind it: the game over the pause menu, the title menu or the pause menu under a screen opened from it, or the parent Options screen under a nested one. It is `peekPlan` in `episodes/episode-1/src/back.ts` (pure: given the screen, the sub-screen and the one under it, what the peek reveals, or none) with `BackPeek` and `peekStyle` in `packages/engine/src/back-peek.ts`. The screens replace one another in a single element, so on `started` the shell lays a copy of the screen being left over itself (`.peek-ghost`: `aria-hidden`, `inert`, `pointer-events: none`) and redraws the real title and overlay as the screen Back goes to (`drawMenus` with the parent as its view), so the parent is already on screen when the gesture ends. The copy follows the finger through `--lf-peek-x`, `--lf-peek-a` and `--lf-peek-feather`, written at most once a frame. The edge the copy leaves behind (the side the swipe came from, `data-peek-edge`) is feathered with a CSS mask on the same element as the transform, so it travels with the slide: it fades from transparent to opaque over `PEEK_FEATHER_FRACTION` (0.14) of the screen's width, growing in over the first 10% of progress, instead of ending in a hard line against the screen behind. It is a plain gradient mask (no filter), absent at progress 0 and with reduced motion, and goes with the copy when the peek ends. The Back button belongs to the screen being left, so the copy carries a clone of it and it slides, fades and feathers with the copy; the real one is redrawn for the screen Back goes to (shown only if that screen has one, as the pause menu and the screens over a menu do) and fades in with it. The copy is given the scroll offsets of the screen and of everything scrollable inside it (`cloneNode` does not copy them), so a screen scrolled on a short display does not jump to its top when the peek starts. The real Back button is inert (`inert`, and `pointer-events: none`) from `started` until the peek ends, so a second touch or an accessibility activation cannot go Back during the gesture. **Backdrop and crossfade:** the scrim is one layer for the whole peek, `.peek-backdrop`, a full-screen element under the copy and the screen Back goes to that never moves, so the strip the copy vacates is dimmed from the first frame instead of showing the game undimmed. For the peek's length the real screens and the copy have no scrim of their own (`background: none`), so scrims never stack. The backdrop holds two layers read from the screens' own computed backgrounds when the peek starts (the leaving screen's, and the destination's, none for the game), so a side gradient stays a gradient and progress 0 equals the real screen; they dissolve into each other with `mix-blend-mode: plus-lighter` at opacities `1 − w` and `w`, where `w` is `backdrop` of `peekStyle` (the complement of the copy's opacity, reaching 1 by progress 0.9). Where the two screens share a scrim the darkness is therefore constant, and over the pause menu it fades to nothing to show the game. The screen Back goes to is drawn behind the copy as content only, with opacity `--lf-peek-parent-a` (`parentOpacity`, the complement of the copy's opacity), and is 0 and `visibility: hidden` below progress 0.15 (`PEEK_PARENT_HIDDEN_BELOW`), so none of its text shows through the nearly whole copy. A cancelled gesture reverses all of it over the same 200 ms as the copy returns; the real screen is then drawn and the copy and the backdrop removed in the same task, so the real scrims come back with no flash. Reduced motion crossfades the same way. `peekStyle(progress, edge, reducedMotion)` slides it up to 30% of the screen width in the swipe's direction (a swipe from the left edge moves it right; with no edge reported it moves right) on an ease-out curve and fades it with a smoothstep to nothing by progress 0.9; progress 0 is the rest state. With reduced motion (the Motion option, or the system setting when Motion is System) it only fades. `cancelled` glides the copy back over 200 ms and then drops it, which puts the real screen back; `invoked` calls `Game.back()` as before and drops the copy in the same task, after the parent has been drawn, so the menu never flashes. The real screens take no taps, keys, pad or commands while a peek is on. The peek always ends: on `cancelled`, on `invoked`, when the screen or sub-screen changes, when the page is hidden, and 3 s after the last gesture event. The title menu itself, the touch controls editor and the story and play screens have no peek (Back on the bare title backgrounds the app with the system's own animation). It plays no sound or haptic of its own; a committed gesture plays the same Back cue as before. Try it without a phone with `?debug&host=fake-android`, which installs `window.__lfPredictiveBack.emit({ type: 'started' | 'progress' | 'cancelled' | 'invoked', progress, swipeEdge })` and reports what the game told the system in `canGoBack`; `e2e/touch-predictive-back.spec.ts` uses it. Not run with a real gesture yet.
- **Android project:** orientation lock (landscape) and fullscreen are native Android settings, not Tauri plugins, so they live in the tracked `src-tauri/gen/android`: `screenOrientation="sensorLandscape"` on the activity, a fullscreen theme with the display cutout in `shortEdges` mode, and `MainActivity` hiding the system bars (immersive, transient bars on swipe). `compileSdk` and `targetSdk` are 36 (the template says 37) and `minSdk` is 26. The tracked project is a fresh `tauri android init` for the real identifier plus `scripts/apply-android-settings.sh`, which makes those edits idempotently and fails if an anchor in the generated files has moved; `init` regenerates the files, so every regeneration (the dev build) runs it again.
- **Native WebView settings:** `MainActivity` overrides `WryActivity.onWebViewCreate(webView)` (Wry calls it from `setWebView` once the WebView exists; `TauriActivity` does not override it) and sets `settings.textZoom = 100` and `settings.mediaPlaybackRequiresUserGesture = false`. The Android WebView applies the system font scale to all text by default, unlike Chrome and Firefox on the same phone, which breaks the pixel-art layout; the game has its own Text size option, so it must not scale. The second setting lets audio start right away in the app, without the first tap a browser needs; the game then creates and resumes its `AudioContext` at boot when it runs in the app (`GameAudio`'s `unlockAtBoot`, set from `isAppHost`), and keeps the first-input handlers as the fallback. The web build still creates no context before a gesture. Both come from `scripts/apply-android-settings.sh`, so the tracked project and the regenerated dev project get them. The probe page reports the root font size (expect 16 px) and the `AudioContext` state before any gesture (expect `running`) to confirm them on a device.
- **Vibration permission:** the manifest does not declare `android.permission.VIBRATE` by hand. The haptics plugin adds it from its own `build.rs` (`tauri_plugin::mobile::update_android_manifest`), and the capability file grants `phone-haptics:default`.
- **Gamepad rumble:** the desktop WebView (WebKitGTK) has no `vibrationActuator`, so the web `gamepadBackend` cannot rumble a pad there. The `gamepad-haptics` plugin (`tauri-plugins-workspace`, `plugins/gamepad-haptics`) plays rumble natively: evdev force feedback on Linux (checked on a DualShock 3), and on Windows and macOS no native backend yet, so the web path plays there. It never reads input, so it sits beside the WebView's Gamepad API. It is the published `tauri-plugin-gamepad-haptics` crate (0.1.0, from crates.io) in the desktop-only target table of `apps/player/src-tauri/Cargo.toml` (which keeps it and `evdev` out of the Android build), registered in `lib.rs` under `#[cfg(desktop)]`, and granted by `capabilities/gamepad-haptics.json` (`gamepad-haptics:default` and `core:event:default` for the pad events, desktop platforms only). The page side is `packages/engine/src/gamepad-plugin.ts`, imported lazily (its own chunk) only on the `tauri-desktop` host. It calls the plugin by raw IPC (`plugin:gamepad-haptics|list_pads`, `play_frames`, `identify`, `stop`) because the game needs only those four commands and importing the published guest (`@liminal-hq/plugin-gamepad-haptics`) would put Tauri's API into the web bundle. It becomes the `controller` target of `GameHaptics`, so the route (a pad is the input device) and the Rumble strength work as on the web. A cue compiles with `compileRumble`, as the web backend does, then goes through the **rumble boost** (below) and the segments go to the pad as motor frames: the strong motor is the plugin's `heavy`, the weak one its `light`, with a silent frame in each gap. Which pad: the native twin (vendor and product from the Web `Gamepad.id`, else the name; the lower slot when two are alike) of the pad the player used last, else the first native pad that can play. The pad list is read at start and again on the plugin's `connected`, `changed` and `disconnected` events. **Rumble boost** (`boostRumble` in `haptic-pattern.ts`, constants `RUMBLE_BOOST`, ranges `RUMBLE_BOOST_LIMITS`): a DualShock 3's heavy motor does nothing below about 0.4 and needs 80 to 100 ms to spin up, and its light motor is on or off (the plugin turns it fully on from 0.5) and a light-only pulse under about 100 ms is not felt, so the compiled cues (50 to 70 ms, quiet) were silent. The boost compiles the cue at full strength, then: a light level under 0.5 folds into the heavy motor (`lightFoldGain` 0.9; 0 turns the fold off) and one at 0.5 or over stays the light kick; the heavy motor goes through `heavyFloor + (1 − heavyFloor) × strong ^ gamma` times `gain` (defaults 0.35, 0.6 and 1), so any segment that plays it clears the dead zone; each segment is at least `minMs` (90) long with a `gapMs` (20) silent gap before the next, which pushes later segments out; the pattern stops at 2000 ms (inside the plugin's 3000 ms total and 2000 ms continuous limits). The Strength setting is applied after the boost, so Light and Medium lower the boosted levels (and may take the heavy motor under its dead zone, which is the player's choice); a light kick that Strength takes under 0.5 folds into the heavy motor. The constants are on `GameHaptics.tune({ boost })` and `tuning().boost`, and are sliders under Pad (boost) in the haptics lab's Compile tab, whose pad lengths show the boosted segments whenever the controller backend boosts. It applies only to a pad `isOnOffLightPad` accepts, which is the DualShock 3 (054c:0268); every other pad, a DualShock 4 included (its two motors are variable and respond at low strengths), plays the plain compile exactly as without the boost, and the backend's `useBoost: false` turns the boost off for all. The plugin now reports `PadInfo.lightBinary` (true for the DualShock 3) and, on such a pad, itself turns a short strong light tap into one full 100 ms pulse, in-between light levels into pulses of at least 100 ms in proportion to the level, and drops levels too faint to fill one (the result says `downgraded` with "Light motor only switches on and off, so it was pulsed to approximate the strength"); light frames of exactly 0 or 1 are untouched. The boost's light fold overlaps with that: with the fold on (the default) the DualShock 3 never sends an in-between light level, because the fold moves it to the heavy motor. Set `lightFoldGain` to 0 (the lab's Light fold slider, or `tune({ boost: { lightFoldGain: 0 } })`) to turn the fold off and let the DS3 receive the plugin's pulses, to compare the two by feel. Defaults are unchanged. TODO: choose the boosted pads by `lightBinary` instead of by the DualShock 3's ids once the two have been compared on hardware. **Plain profile:** every other native pad (a DualShock 4, an Xbox pad) keeps its compiled levels and starts exactly as compiled, but a segment shorter than `minMs` (`RUMBLE_PLAIN`, default 90) is lengthened to it, never into the next segment (so a hum made of short slices keeps its length), because a very short pulse is not felt on some pads (a DualShock 4 does not feel a heavy-only 60 ms pulse at 0.6; 90 ms at 0.6 and 60 ms at 1.0 it does). It is the Pad (plain) slider in the lab and `tune({ plain })`; `GameHaptics.controllerProfile()` says which profile the pad in use plays. **Menu cues on a DualShock 3:** the boost lifts every cue to the heavy floor and the menu's 1.5 times boost then saturated them all at 1.0, so a menu cue (`compileMenuRumble`) sets the heavy motor to `0.8 × relative × Rumble`, where `relative` is the cue's strongest intensity over 0.5 (the loudest menu cue, `ui.select`), and never under 0.35 while it plays: at Strong move 0.48, select 0.8, back 0.64, reject 0.72, toggle on 0.8 and toggle off 0.72 (at Medium 0.36, 0.60, 0.48, 0.54, 0.60 and 0.54); the backend learns a cue is a menu cue from the `ctx.ui` argument of `play`. **Menu cues** go to the phone's vibrator as before, but where the phone cannot play (a desktop has no vibrator) and a pad is the device in use they go to the controller at the Rumble strength times the menu boost, so menus can be felt on a pad (`GameHaptics.uiTarget`); with no pad in use they are dropped. **Two pads:** the cue plays on the native twin of the pad with the latest input (`InputManager.activePad()`), matched by vendor and product when the id has them and otherwise by name, where an equal name wins and a "Sony " prefix is ignored (a USB DualShock 3 is "Sony PLAYSTATION(R)3 Controller", over Bluetooth "PLAYSTATION(R)3 Controller"). In the haptics lab the header's Auto, Phone, Pad and Off pick the audition target; Pad is playable whenever the controller backend can play, including a native pad the Gamepad API does not report as rumbling, and the status line names the pad (name, connection and plugin id) and explains an unavailable target only when it is the one chosen (in Auto, only when neither can play). **Rumble per pad model:** the Rumble row (Options > Haptics) shows and sets the strength of the pad in use, with its name ("Strong, Wireless Controller"), keyed by `vendorId:productId` (`padModelKey`, `1356:616` for a DualShock 3) of the native twin of `activePad()` (on the web, of the Web pad's id when it carries them). The levels are saved in `rumblePads` in `lf-ep1-options-v1`, beside the old single `rumble`, which stays the default for a model with no entry (an old save needs no migration). A DualShock 3 with no entry starts on Medium, or on the default when that is lower (Off or Light); every other model starts on the default (Strong). With no pad used yet the row changes the default, and stepping it plays a bonk on the pad. The strength follows the pad in use when the player changes pads. Reset puts Rumble on Strong and drops every model's saved level. **One writer per pad:** a pad found natively is played only natively, and a pad the plugin cannot address (one it does not list, or lists without write access) is left to the web backend, which reports it unavailable when its Gamepad has no actuator, which `gamepad-plugin.ts` keeps inside as its fallback, so the two never play the same pad; with no pad at all it does nothing. A native pad that can play also shows the Haptics screen and its Rumble row, which the Gamepad API cannot say on this WebView. The haptics page (`apps/player/haptics.html`) has a "Gamepad rumble" section: the pads the plugin and the webview see, Identify, the game's cues on the controller target, a raw `playFrames` box with presets, the guest's `createBackend` (register, trigger, `setMasterScale`, `setMaxTier`, stop), a `resolvePad` box, and a log of every call and pad event that copies as JSON. The section uses the plugin's own guest package, `@liminal-hq/plugin-gamepad-haptics` (a `^0.1.0` dependency of `apps/player` from npm, imported only by the haptics page). With `?debug` (or the dev server) the section's tools are on `window.__lfGamepad`. On Linux the plugin needs write access to `/dev/input/event*`, which a desktop's `uaccess` rule normally gives. Not yet run on a pad through the whole app: the plugin was checked on a DualShock 3 on its own, and this wiring is covered by unit tests with a fake plugin.
- **Logs:** `tauri-plugin-log` carries Rust `log` records and the page's console output, uncaught errors and unhandled rejections in one stream (stdout and logcat, a rotated file in the app's log directory, and the devtools console for Rust's records), with Threshold's `[time %:z][LEVEL][target]` format. Debug builds log at Debug and release builds at Info. The page side is `packages/engine/src/tauri-log.ts`, started by each page only inside the app, with a page prefix; there is no compatibility layer. The `log:default` permission is in its own `capabilities/logging.json`. Where to read it on each platform is in [TAURI_SPIKE.md](TAURI_SPIKE.md#reading-the-logs).

## Identity
- Application id `ca.liminalhq.lieutenantfizz`, with a side-by-side dev build `ca.liminalhq.lieutenantfizz.dev` and its own launcher icon, as Cadence does with `ca.liminalhq.cadence.dev`.
- The dev build follows Cadence and Threshold: the tracked `gen/android` stays the real app, and a dev build deletes it, runs `tauri android init --config src-tauri/tauri.conf.dev.json` (the `.dev` identifier and the 'Lieutenant Fizz Dev' name), re-applies the Android settings and stamps the dev ribbon icon with `tauri icon` (`scripts/prepare-android-dev.sh`). GitHub Actions runs those steps in the `tauri-ci-mobile` container and is the default way to get an APK. The one sanctioned local path is `bun run build:android:dev` (`scripts/build-android-dev.sh`), which runs the same steps in the `tauri-dev-mobile` container, refuses to run when `gen/android` has uncommitted changes, always restores `gen/android` to HEAD afterwards (and exits non-zero if that restore does not leave the tree clean, even when the build succeeded), builds one ABI (aarch64; `FIZZ_DEV_TARGET=universal` for all), caps parallelism (`FIZZ_DEV_JOBS`, default 4), puts the heavy directories (the Rust build output, the cargo registry and the Gradle cache) under `FIZZ_DEV_SCRATCH` when it is set, so they can live on another disk (the Android SDK and NDK extras always stay on the Docker named volume `fizz-android-sdk-extras`, in Docker storage, because a host directory would start empty and hide the image's SDK and NDK), and copies the APK to `FIZZ_DEV_APK_DIR` (default `~/fizz-dev-builds`) and installs it when a device is attached. No other local Android build, emulator or `tauri android dev` is used.

## Tooling and CI
- **Two Cargo workspaces:** the root workspace holds the simulation (`crates/sim` and the episode game crates) and excludes `apps/player/src-tauri`, which is its own workspace root with its own `Cargo.lock` and release profile. The app's heavy dependencies and its WebKitGTK and GTK system libraries stay out of the simulation's checks, so `cargo test --workspace`, `bun run test:rust` and `bun run validate` are unchanged. The app's Rust is checked by its own job, `.github/workflows/ci-app.yml`, which runs on pull requests that touch `apps/player/` or the shared `rust-toolchain.toml` (the app inherits that pinned toolchain) in the desktop CI image: `cargo fmt --check`, `cargo clippy --locked` and `cargo test --locked` on the desktop target, with a stub frontend directory (`tauri::generate_context!` needs `apps/player/dist` to exist). `bun run check:app-rust` and `bun run test:app-rust` run the same checks locally; they are not part of `validate`.
- **`bun run validate` does not build the app.** The Android build (SDK, NDK, signing, an AAB for the Play Store) is its own heavy workflow, like the e2e job. **GitHub Actions is the default place to build Android** (`.github/workflows/android-apk.yml` for debug builds, started by hand or on pushes to `feat/tauri-player*` branches; the signed release build is in `release.yml`, see Releases). The only sanctioned local build is `scripts/build-android-dev.sh` (see Identity); there is no local `tauri android dev`, emulator or bare Gradle run.
- **Generated files:** Prettier, ESLint and the licence-header check ignore `gen/android`.
- **Build order:** `bun run build:wasm`, then `bun run build:app` (`scripts/build-app.sh`), which builds the player pages and then each episode with `--base ./` into `apps/player/dist/episode-N/`, the app's `frontendDist`. Only this script passes `--base`, so the Pages build keeps the episode's own base. Prettier, ESLint and the licence-header check skip the generated `apps/player/src-tauri/gen/`.
- The Pages deploy is unaffected.

## Releases
A `vX.Y.Z` tag makes one release with the Linux packages and a signed Android build on it. Windows, macOS and the upload to Google Play come later: Windows and macOS will attach to the same release, and Play gets its own step (a service account and a track) once the first release has been checked by hand. The Android debug workflow (`android-apk.yml`) drafts debug APKs under its own `android-debug-<run>-<attempt>` tags, which never share a tag with a `vX.Y.Z` release.

- **Cutting a release:** the app's version is in three places that must agree: `version` in `apps/player/src-tauri/tauri.conf.json`, `[package] version` in `apps/player/src-tauri/Cargo.toml` (with `Cargo.lock`) and `version` in `apps/player/package.json`. Bump them in one commit and write the version's release notes in `docs/releases/<version>.md` (the release's body, with GitHub's list of changes after it; a missing file falls back to a one-line body), merge it to `main`, then tag that commit `v` plus the version (`v0.1.0` for 0.1.0) and push the tag. `bun scripts/check-release-version.ts` checks that the three agree, and `bun scripts/check-release-version.ts --tag v0.1.0` also checks the tag; it prints the version or fails naming the file that differs, so it is the dry run before tagging. It also fails if `tauri.conf.json` sets `bundle.android.versionCode` (see Android versions below). The tag must be `vX.Y.Z` or `vX.Y.Z-beta.N` (a pre-release suffix marks the release as a pre-release).
- **What the workflow does:** `.github/workflows/release.yml` ("Release") runs on a pushed `v*` tag. Its jobs are `prepare` (repeats that version check against the tag and refuses a tag whose commit is not on `main`), `draft-release`, `build-linux`, `build-android`, `package-appimage`, `assemble` and `attach-to-release`. `build-linux` builds the `.deb` and `.rpm` for x86_64 and aarch64 (GitHub's `ubuntu-24.04` and `ubuntu-24.04-arm` runners, in the shared `tauri-ci-desktop` image, with `bun run build:wasm`, `bun run build:app` and `bun run tauri build --bundles deb,rpm`), `package-appimage` repackages each `.deb` as an AppImage with the org's reusable `package-arch-appimage.yml`, `build-android` builds the signed Android release (below), and `assemble` writes `SHA256SUMS` over every file; `attach-to-release` attaches all of it to a release named "Lieutenant Fizz vX.Y.Z" with the notes from `docs/releases/<version>.md` and generated notes. If a draft for the tag already exists it is reused and the files are replaced; a release that is already published is left alone and the run fails.
- **The files on the release:** `Lieutenant-Fizz_<version>_<arch>.deb`, `Lieutenant-Fizz-<version>-1.<arch>.rpm` and the AppImage (its own name) for both architectures, with no spaces; `lieutenant-fizz-v<versionName>-<versionCode>.apk` and `.aab` (the signed universal APK and the app bundle); `lieutenant-fizz-mapping-v<versionName>-<versionCode>.txt` (the R8 mapping, to read a release crash trace); `lieutenant-fizz-native-debug-symbols-v<versionName>-<versionCode>.zip` (the native debug symbols, below); and `SHA256SUMS`.
- **It is published, after the files are on it:** the release is created as a draft so it is never public half-filled, and the last step of `attach-to-release` publishes it once every file is attached (a `-beta.N` tag stays a pre-release). Check the dry run and the version first, because a pushed tag releases without a review step, and a published release cannot be rebuilt by the workflow (it fails on one that is already public). Starting the workflow by hand (Actions, Release) builds everything, signs the Android build and uploads the files as the `fizz-player-release` workflow artefact (the Android files are also in `android-release-assets`), and publishes nothing, unless its `release_tag` input names an existing tag; tick `draft` there to leave that release a draft for review. A run without a tag is the dry run of the whole workflow.
- **Android build and signing:** `build-android` runs in the shared `tauri-ci-mobile` image as root (`--user 0`), adds the four Android Rust targets, builds the WASM and the frontend, then writes the upload keystore from the org secrets `ANDROID_UPLOAD_KEYSTORE_BASE64` (the keystore, base64), `ANDROID_UPLOAD_KEY_ALIAS` and `ANDROID_UPLOAD_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD` (selected to this repository) into `.ci/signing/` and a `keystore.properties` (`keyAlias`, `password`, `storeFile`) in `apps/player/src-tauri/gen/android/`, and checks that the keystore opens. The tracked `gen/android/app/build.gradle.kts` reads that file and signs the `release` build type only when it exists, so debug and dev builds (and `scripts/build-android-dev.sh`) are never signed with it. The job builds with `bun run android:build --apk --aab` (with the two Cargo variables of the next item set), runs `jarsigner -verify` on every output, collects the files and removes the key in an `always()` step. Both `keystore.properties` and `.ci/` are git-ignored.
- **Native debug symbols:** the Rust library is built unstripped for the Android release only, so Play can symbolicate native crashes. The repository's `[profile.release]` keeps `strip = true` (the desktop packages stay small); the `build-android` build step sets `CARGO_PROFILE_RELEASE_STRIP=none` and `CARGO_PROFILE_RELEASE_DEBUG=line-tables-only` in its environment, which Cargo reads as overrides of that profile for that one build (`none` keeps the symbol table, `line-tables-only` adds file and line numbers without full debug information). The tracked Gradle project sets `ndk { debugSymbolLevel = "FULL" }` on the release build type, so Android Gradle Plugin strips the library it ships in the APK and AAB (about 6 MB per ABI, the same as before) and writes the full copy to `BUNDLE-METADATA/com.android.tools.build.debugsymbols/<abi>/liblieutenant_fizz_player_lib.so.dbg` in the AAB (about 44 MB per ABI, a few MB compressed). That only works because the template's `keepDebugSymbols` patterns are scoped to the debug variants in `build.gradle.kts`: inside the debug build type they applied to every build, which kept the release library unstripped and left AGP with nothing to extract. `scripts/apply-android-settings.sh` makes both changes on a regenerated project. The workflow then zips those `.dbg` files, ABI folders at the root, as `lieutenant-fizz-native-debug-symbols-v<versionName>-<versionCode>.zip` (attached to the release and in `SHA256SUMS`), and fails the job if the AAB carries none. **In Play Console:** an AAB that contains the symbols needs no extra step, since Play reads them from the bundle on upload; the zip is the same data for a manual upload (Test and release, App bundle explorer, the release's Downloads tab, Native debug symbols, Upload) or for a Play upload that cannot read the bundle's metadata. Upload it for the same version code as the bundle.
- **Key custody:** the upload key is the one Threshold uses, kept in `/home/scott/threshold-keys` on the maintainer's machine (`upload-keystore.jks`, `keystore.properties` and the public `upload-cert.pem`). GitHub's secrets cannot be read back, so keep that directory backed up somewhere safe (an encrypted drive or a password manager): a lost upload key can only be replaced through a Play Console reset request. The certificate's SHA-256 is `F0:8F:4B:05:E0:62:8B:A4:0E:ED:F3:7C:F6:24:69:34:88:4B:F4:FA:07:1D:8B:17:68:91:91:56:3F:D7:1B:5B`; compare it with the upload certificate in Play Console (Test and release, App signing) and with `apksigner verify --print-certs` on the release APK, which prints it without colons.
- **Play App Signing:** with Play App Signing, Google re-signs what Play serves with its own app signing key, so an app installed from Play carries a different certificate from the upload key; only the APK from the release (and the bundle Play receives) is signed with the upload key. The release is not uploaded to Play by the workflow.
- **Android versions:** Tauri writes `gen/android/app/tauri.properties` during the build: `versionName` is the `tauri.conf.json` version and `versionCode` is derived from it (major × 1,000,000 + minor × 1,000 + patch, so 0.1.0 gives 1000 and 0.4.0 gives 4000), which `build.gradle.kts` reads. The workflow takes the file names from that file and fails if `versionName` differs from the tag's version. No explicit `versionCode` is set in `tauri.conf.json` or Gradle (the version check fails if one is). Play needs a strictly increasing `versionCode`, which this scheme gives as long as the semver only goes up and the minor and patch components stay under 1000. Uploading the same version to Play again needs a bump.
- **Package metadata:** the Linux packages take their listing from `bundle` in `tauri.conf.json`: `shortDescription` and `longDescription` (the summary and the body that a package installer shows), `homepage`, `license`, `publisher` and `copyright` (the maintainer), `category` (`ArcadeGame`, the desktop menu's Games section) and `linux.deb.section` (`games`). Change them there, not in the workflow.
- **Unsigned desktop packages:** the Linux packages carry no signature (no package or repository signing key), so `SHA256SUMS` is the only integrity check, and it is attached to the same release it covers. The Android files are signed with the upload key and are also in `SHA256SUMS`.
- **Debug tooling:** the release profile builds without `debug_assertions`, so the MCP bridge plugin (`tauri-plugin-mcp-bridge`) is compiled in but never registered, as in every release build.
- **Controller rumble on Linux:** native rumble opens `/dev/input/event*` for writing, which a desktop's `uaccess` udev rule grants to the person at the console. A system without one (a minimal install, a remote session) plays no rumble until the user can write to the pad's event node. Nothing in the packages changes that.

## Phasing
The mobile experience on the web comes first, because it is an extension of the game as it is today and needs no new app. It is planned in slices in [MOBILE_PLAN.md](MOBILE_PLAN.md). The app work follows.
1. **Touch controls, the phone layout and basic haptics on the web** (MOBILE_PLAN.md, slices 0 to 9). They are web UI, so the website and phone browsers get them, and everything below reuses them.
2. **Spike (built and run on a Pixel device once; measurements still to do):** `apps/player` runs the existing episode build in the Tauri WebView, and a probe page reports how WebGL2, WASM, audio, the gamepad and the rest behave. Findings go in [TAURI_SPIKE.md](TAURI_SPIKE.md).
3. **Shared options and the launch contract:** the shared options key with per-episode overrides, `parseLaunch()`, and embedded mode in Episode 1.
4. **The launcher:** the carousel, App settings, Saves export and import, and the demo-strip art.
5. **Android lifecycle and release:** orientation and fullscreen, the Back button, pausing on background, the icon set, signing, the heavy CI job and the dev-build script.
6. **Desktop extras:** window mode, rumble and the fullscreen scale.
7. **Haptics** once the plugin upgrade and the touch controls exist.

## Open questions
- Is the Play Store a goal, or is installing a signed APK or AAB enough for now?
- Will any episode ever be paid or separately priced? That is the one thing that would favour separate apps.
- **Placeholder content in the artboards:** the artboards estimate content (episode titles, save values, the art), and the current implementation wins where they differ.

- **Pixel scale in the launcher:** the fullscreen design scales the art 4.8×. The type and art rule everywhere else is whole-pixel scales. Should the launcher follow the same rule (an integer scale with margins), or is a fractional scale acceptable for the demo strip behind the menu?
- **Does the demo strip animate beyond Ben running?** The art generator draws a static strip with Ben's two run frames and the soda cursor.

## Decisions
- One app for all episodes rather than an app per episode (bundles are small, settings and saves are shared).
- Tauri rather than a PWA wrapper, so saves live in app storage that the browser cannot evict, and native plugins (haptics, Back gesture) are first class. The web build keeps working as before.
- The launcher is the episode's title screen (design 1a), not a hub that hands off.
- The launcher is pre-drawn art and loads no game code.
- Episodes are launched by navigating to their own page with a launch contract, not by mounting them in the launcher.
- Options are global with per-episode overrides.
- Icon 2g (Fizz ring · Zargoth).
- The website stays static HTML.
